# Source-faithful prototype

1. Map the source parts, assembly method, already manufactured pieces, owned inventory and machine limits before changing geometry. Preserve the source look and load path. Record each necessary departure and its reason.
2. Start with the simplest adequate workshop attachment: zip ties, double-sided tape, ordinary or electrical tape, small glue joints and flat stock. Use accessible wrapping paths, broad mounting faces and simple stops. Add a printed bracket, precision cradle, cover or fastener only for a named need that these methods cannot meet, such as shaft alignment, impact loads or service access. Match the joint to heat, vibration, surface bonding and the carried load; keep cells uncompressed and glue out of moving parts. Count pieces, ties, supports and fitting operations. Prefer local fitting over a new architecture.
3. Establish the exact hardware model and interfaces from the local inventory, source CAD and manufacturer drawing. Model families and broad specifications do not establish a bolt pattern. Distinguish pitch-circle diameter, adjacent spacing and diagonal spacing. Check the actual part when sources conflict. Use existing accessible joints that carry the load; name the smallest missing measurement rather than designing around unmeasured future hardware. Prefer compatible ready-made electronics. Check their onboard circuitry before adding resistors, capacitors or board modifications; each addition needs a specific electrical reason. Fit the structure around practical modules and soldered assemblies instead of forcing miniature circuits into arbitrary CAD reservations.
4. Release one numbered cutting and printing set with a short illustrated assembly sequence, placement, every electrical connection and checks. Show intermediate fits, not just the finished model.
5. Compare every final part with its source, especially after booleans. Prove exact cut, print and guide files by readback and hashes. Label predictions as predictions; physical fit, strength, balance and flight remain real-world checks.

6. Separate nominal CAD dimensions from measured stock and hardware. Carry thickness, process error and the intended fit through both mating parts; changing a plate thickness without its receiving slots can make a valid model unbuildable. Derive a clearance or interference from the process and functional fit, not a universal allowance. Use generous clearance for taped or tied electronics; reserve precision fits for interfaces that need them. Qualify representative critical mating fits before manufacturing the whole set; dry-fit the assembly before bonding or closure. Preserve work already cut or printed when an evidenced local repair is sufficient.
7. A change from wood or metal to print needs a material and load-path decision, not just an STL extrusion. Explain how thrust, torque, clamp pressure, vibration and heat reach the retained structure; select orientation, walls and infill accordingly. Account for layer separation, creep, warping and the actual printer/material capability. Datasheet specimen properties do not rate the finished assembly. State the physical bond, clamp, thermal and load tests still needed.
8. Prove a builder can identify each part, insert it in the intended order, reach every fastener with a hand or tool, route and secure the wires, and service the equipment after closure. Use the full screw/washer/plate stack and actual safe thread engagement, including hidden shafts, bosses and winding clearance. Show orientation and accessible bond/retention faces; a collision-free model is not evidence of an attached joint.

For authorized skill maintenance, keep transferable corrections in the existing portable `agent-config/skills` sources, follow its installation manifest and synchronize the relevant installed copies, including an active project copy that would shadow the global skill. Verify their hashes agree; preserve unrelated local changes and machine-specific state.
